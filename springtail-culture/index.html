Springtails: how to culture them and seed a new vivarium

Written November 2026 by Lone Star Leaf Litter, the family business that keeps this archive online.

We sell Texas live oak leaf litter. Leaves get one short mention below, as cover for springtails inside the tank. Nothing else in this article involves anything we sell.

What springtails are for

Springtails are tiny, wingless, six-legged animals that live in damp soil and leaf litter. Most of the ones kept in the hobby are white and only a millimetre or two long. They get their name from a forked tail folded under the body that flicks them into the air when they are disturbed.

They eat mold, fungus and fine decaying matter, and they leave living plants alone. In a humid vivarium that makes them the first line of the cleanup crew: they graze the fuzz that grows on new wood, old food and droppings. Isopods handle the bigger pieces, which is why the two are usually kept together.

They are also food. Dart frogs and other small animals hunt them, and they are small enough for very young froglets. For adult frogs, treat them as a snack between feedings and not as a main diet.

Because the animals eat them, a tank population rises and falls. A culture kept outside the tank lets you top it up whenever you want.

Charcoal or a soil-type mix

Keepers use two kinds of culture, and each has a job.

  • Charcoal. Lumps of horticultural or hardwood charcoal sitting in a little water. It stays clean, does not break down, and is the easiest to harvest from because springtails float and wet charcoal does not. It is also reported to be less prone to mites. This is the usual choice for a long-term culture.
  • Soil-type mix. Coconut fiber or a damp vivarium substrate. You harvest by scooping some of the mix straight into the tank, which is handy for seeding. The cost is that a damp organic mix is more likely to pick up mites, and every scoop takes media out of the culture.

If you are starting out, charcoal is the simplest to get right, and the rest of this article assumes it.

Setting up a charcoal culture

You need a starter culture, a plastic container with a lid, charcoal and water.

  1. Container. A plastic shoe box or a food tub of similar size is the common choice. A taller container means fewer springtails jumping out when you open it.
  2. Charcoal. Use plain lump or horticultural charcoal with no additives, never barbecue briquettes with lighter fluid or binders. Rinse off the dust and fill the container about halfway.
  3. Water. Add dechlorinated, distilled or reverse osmosis water to the bottom. Keepers use anywhere from a quarter of an inch to about an inch. The point is a wet base and humid air, with most of the charcoal standing above the waterline.
  4. Springtails. Tip the starter culture in. More is better: a culture started with a good handful of animals gets going sooner than one started with a few dozen.
  5. Lid. The culture has to breathe. Either leave the lid closed but not airtight, make a few very small holes covered with fine mesh, or keep it sealed and open it for a few minutes every couple of days. A sealed culture that is forgotten can crash.

Keep it at normal room temperature. About 65 to 80°F is the range usually given, and reproduction slows when it runs hotter. Keep the container out of direct sun.

Feeding the culture

Springtails mostly eat the mold that grows on their food, so a little fuzz on yesterday's feeding is normal.

The most widely used food is plain brewer's yeast, or a commercial springtail food. Give a very light sprinkle every few days, and skip a feeding if the last one is still sitting there. A few grains of uncooked rice are another common option and last longer between feedings.

Keepers disagree about other foods. Fish flakes, dried mushroom, oatmeal and vegetable scraps all have their users. Other breeders avoid them, grain-based foods above all, because they are blamed for bringing in mites. If you want the lowest risk, stay with yeast.

Overfeeding is the usual way a culture is lost. Too much food spoils, fouls the air in the container and invites pests. Feed less than seems right, and mist lightly with clean water when you open the lid if the upper charcoal looks dry.

Expect about a month before a new culture is producing heavily. Leave it alone until then.

Mites and crashes

Mites are the main pest. They look rounder than springtails and move slowly, and they do not jump. Once they take over a culture there is no dependable way to remove them, and most keepers discard it and start again from a clean source.

Prevention is cheaper:

  • feed lightly and stick to low-risk foods
  • keep cultures away from fruit fly cultures, grain and pet food
  • use clean tools and wash your hands between containers
  • run at least two cultures in separate places, so one loss is not all of them

Starting a second culture is easy. Set up a new container the same way and pour a harvest from the first one into it.

If a culture goes quiet without mites, check the three usual causes: it dried out, it was overfed, or it went too long without fresh air.

Harvesting

Add clean water to the culture until it rises around the charcoal. The springtails float up and gather on the surface. Pour the water and its raft of springtails where you want them, then let the level in the culture drop back to normal.

For a small dose, lift out one piece of charcoal and tap it over the tank.

Never strip a culture. Take part of the population and give it time to recover, which can take weeks after a heavy harvest.

Seeding a new vivarium

The best time to add springtails is while you are building the tank or soon after, before any animals arrive. Suppliers commonly suggest three to five weeks of lead time. That gives the population a chance to spread through the substrate with nothing eating it, and the mold that often appears in a new tank gives it plenty to eat.

Keepers differ on the exact method, and both of these work:

  • mix a soil-type culture into the top of the substrate before you plant, without packing the substrate down
  • plant first, then pour a charcoal harvest over the substrate, wood and background

If you pour, use only as much water as it takes to carry them, and watch the level in the drainage layer so the soil above does not end up sitting in water.

There is no exact number to aim for. A few dozen will colonize a small tank in time, and several hundred give a larger tank a quicker start. Cover matters more than the count. A layer of leaf litter over the substrate, along with bark and wood resting on the soil, holds moisture and gives springtails somewhere to breed out of reach.

In a tank that already has animals, release springtails under the leaves or into a planted corner, and repeat every few weeks if the animals hunt them hard. Keep the culture going after the tank is established, as insurance against a population that has been eaten down.
