I really think its a fowler's because the Dorsal spots are too well defined.
Here is from my copy of A key to Amphibians and Reptiles of the Continental United States and Canada.
16a. Dorsal spots relatively indistinct (appearing almost "washed-out") and encircling one to several warts; range largely restricted to the Great Plains and arid Southwest U.S. -------B. woodhousii

16b. Dorsal spots usually large and well-defined, frequently six in number, and encircling three or more warts; range largely east of the Great Plains ----- B. fowlerii

Notice it does not say restricted to east of the Great Plains. The spots on your toad look pretty well defined to me, compare it to the spots of some photos of other B. woodhousii's.

Notice it does not say restricted to east of the Great Plains. The spots on your toad look pretty well defined to me, compare it to the spots of some photos of other B. woodhousii's.
The problem is that we are looking at a YOUNG toad. Trust me. It is a woodhouse's toad. It looks EXACTLY like the young one's I've collected around the zoo.

The spots the key is referring to are the larger spots that adult toads have. Right now they are hidden by the little red spots on your toad. As it gets older the red spots will fade and almost disappear, leaving loosely defined large spots behind. In other words, the little red spots are not what the key is referring too when it mentions well defined spots.
