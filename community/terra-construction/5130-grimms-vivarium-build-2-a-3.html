crete in general is a very resilient product. it does have it's flaws though. sealing can help with a lot of things although it's not absolutely necessary in almost all cases. it can help keep the crete from over drying and cracking, getting too wet and falling apart, wearing down from everyday use and most importantly... crete is very porous and very few crete products will actually HOLD water. if you were to use it for a reservoir i'd definitely seal it. other than making it hold water though there's virtually no reason sealing is absolutely necessary as there are ways to work around its other downfalls fairly easily. the ph thing for example can usually be remedied with a few good rinses and/or a quick vinegar bath/spritz to counter act the lye.

the best way to cure concrete is moist. if it dries during the initial curing phase it cracks just about every time, especially when poured thin like we'd do for our vivs. for this it's best to keep a spray bottle handy to give it a quick mist down before it becomes completely dry.

the tightbond does not create a thick gooey looking mess under your b/g cover layer that you can see when you look into the viv. not only that as things fall off over time you won't be exposing a faded, grayed out, murky layer of silicone that's left behind if you use the tightbond. also your cover material can be mixed into the tightbond as well as pressed into it so when the outer layer rots or falls off you're left with the same stuff... just slightly shinier.

my crete b/g for my crabitat is not sealed nor will the one for the beardie be... unless by the off chance i decide to add a water feature for him. at that point only the water feature will be sealed.

if you do do crete for a b/g and seal it, then add broms at a later date it might be better to just mount them or drill or cut the crete with a grinder instead of trying to poke it. i'd be more weary of cracking a thin sheet of crete than cutting into my sealer. using one of these methods should keep you from having to worry about that.

one thing i notice about crete work in vivs is most people try to add details in their b/g before adding the crete. this will not work out well. best bet is to get overall shapes and add your fine details to your crete itself with a blade or something. you'll get your details that way and not waste your time doing something intricate that'll just wash out as you cover it over.

if you're already using tightbond i don't see why you can't just mix some of that in when you mix up your last couple coats of crete to seal it from the inside. anything you put on the outside will cause a sheen unless you sprinkle sand on it while it's still wet... even a little then. there's a bunch of things you could use to seal it and i'm sure you'll get suggestions, i'm just sure i'll miss a whole lot if i listed things right now. i haven't been clear minded in over a week.

concrete can be wiped off of glass quite easily while you're working with it but if you're concerned, by all means tape it up. any small clumps that dry on the glass can leave a bothersome stain for a bit that just irks me to no end... not that it won't eventually come off.

with the silicone... use it in smaller sections, pump it in there quickly and smooth it with your glove hand quickly and press the coco in using large handfuls and decent pressure with your clean hand.

if you patched over existing silicone with silicone it's likely you'll leak again.

First, I don't think you guys are understanding me on how the concrete binder or titebond is used. You don't use it like silicone, you mix it together with whatever you want to use for the BG (sand, peat, coco fiber, etc) and then spread it on similar to concrete, except you do it in a single layer.

I was referring to the concrete binder method, not concrete. The purpose of letting it dry and then wetting it when you use the binder method is to leach out the excess binder. This makes the structure stronger.

And again:
Quote:
Froggy have you ever tried the Titebond method yourself? If it gets hard as a rock it doesnt seem like I would be able to stick Broms into the foam. What makes the Titebond method look more realistic then the silicone method if I use the same co-co and moss on both?
No, I haven't tried the titebond. I have, however, tried the binder. It works the same way, it's just that the titebond is waterproof, allowing you to use it on water features.

If you want to put broms in the foam, you'll have to use staples or glue, or put them in before you cover the BG in the mixture.

It looks MUCH more realistic. It doesn't look like you glued stuff onto a piece of foam. It looks much more realistic. With the silicone method, so many things can go wrong, and even when nothing does, it still erodes, especially if exposed to running water or constant misting (or even occasional high pressure misting). The coco fiber/moss/whatever is going to wash off, and then you'll just see the icky silicone underneath. It's time consuming, you have to work fast, and it takes a LONG time to cure. The binder/titebond method can even replace the concrete method, since you can use it with sand. Using sand will make it turn out just like concrete, without having to leach it or keep it moist so it will properly cure. With the binder/titebond method, if it cracks while drying, you can just patch it, I'm not sure how well that works with concrete. I'll see if I can get a picture of the small piece I am using with the binder mix on it. It just looks sooooooooo much more natural than silicone.

Now, just to clarify (since I think you guys got it wrong to begin with), with the bonder/titebond method, you MIX the binder/titebond with peat/coco fiber/sand/soil/etc and if too thick, a little water, then you spread it on your foam (it needs to have the skin removed first if you're using GS) and shape it like you would with concrete. It sticks in one layer because the binder and titebond are both essentially glues (and the titebond IS a glue, lol). It's much fast than the other methods; you can dry it overnight with a fan, then wet it throughly to leach the excess binder (shouldn't be necessary with the titebond, another reason I like it), then dry it again. After that, it's ready for plants and inhabitants. Two days max, from start to finish (not counting foam curing times, of course).

You can also mix in ground moss/moss spores and they should eventually grow as long as you keep the humidity high and mist the BG from time to time. If you're using a fogger, that's even better.

Please let me know if I need to clarify that better. I really think you guys had it all wrong to begin with. You don't put the glue on the foam and then press anything into it, you mix it in a bucket.

Google flevopol to see examples and to get a recipe (I do have one somewhere, I'll just have to locate it). Beck, the guy in your favorite link used this method... I didn't think you'd misunderstand what I was talking about...
