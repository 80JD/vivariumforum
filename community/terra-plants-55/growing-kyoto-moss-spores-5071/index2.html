I don't think that's a good comparison at all. It's not that difficult, it just takes a bit of experimentation to figure out the best way to do it. Just because you don't want to put in the effort doesn't mean that others don't. If none of us experiment to come up with new techniques, then no one will benefit in the future and the hobby will stagnate. Please don't think I'm being rude, I assure that isn't my object. Coming up with ways to easily grow a variety of mosses in a vivarium setting is a big interest of mine. I'm not content to grow a single type of moss. I need variety, I want to bring the variety of mosses I see outside my door into my house where I can closely examine them every day, if I want to.

I'm actually having great success, so far, with some moss I transplanted into my CWD vive. It's looking lush and stays very hydrated, and doesn't seem to be suffering from the high temperature (in the 90's) or bright light. It is under the output from my humidifier, so it stays hydrated 24/7. I think that this is the key when transplanting moss. Hopefully we'll be able to finish our discussion on moss and the moss mat system and techniques developed by Mr. Smith sometime this week.

If your spores still aren't sprouting, perhaps you should expose them to a more acidic environment to break down the coating on the spores so that they can germinate. You could take a small bit of them and soak them for a couple of minutes in some vinegar, or sprinkle a pinch or two on the substrate you are using (in a separate container) and mist them with diluted vinegar (you could also do a bit with straight vinegar and see if that changes the result). Also, it seems to me that the best growing medium for moss spores is moss. I get this from my observations out in my yard. I find lots of mossy places with young mosses of different types mixed in with more established mosses. Perhaps you can get some untreated dried moss to propagate your spores in. They definitely require an acidic environment to dissolve the coating on the spores. The coating keeps the spores from growing until they are in the correct environment.
