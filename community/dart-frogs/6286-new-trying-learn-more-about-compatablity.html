Most tinc morphs, including azureus, are territorial and the females, in particular, will fight and eat one another's eggs. Having said that, I know quite a few folks who have tinc trios and do fine. Azureus are fantastic beginning frogs as long as you don't mind keeping them in pairs as adults. However, the tank you describe sounds like about 100 gallons, which is large enough for a couple of pairs if you can construct sufficient visual barriers so each can have its own territory.

Auratus behave similarly, but I understand they aren't quite as territorial as tincs. I only have a breeding pair, so I have no experience with groups of adults.

For thumbnails, you could probably have three pairs of some species in a tank that size and be fine. You'd also likely have a lot of froglets after a while if it was planted with plenty of bromiliads.

I just got my first leucomelas and can't speak from experience, but I understand that they do well in groups when you have a large enclosure.

As far as compatability goes, if you mix any Dendrobates species, or morphs within species, you can get hybrids, which are frowned upon in the hobby.

I don't know if there is any danger in mixing thumbnails with ground dwellers in a large enclosure.

Overall, if you are less interested in breeding and more interested in a nice display tank, you could experiment with a group of frogs. Just watch for weight loss in given specimens, which can be an indicator that there are conflicts and stress.

Good luck.

On the subject of lighting, it depends on the plants, of course. But, two 48" high output T-5 6,700k bulbs would probably be enough for any plants you want to grow. If you have metal halides, then you probably have had a reef tank. The lighting requirements are similar, but you are correct that the halides would produce way too much heat. Most dart species like it in the mid to upper 70s and with high humidity. This means little no ventilation, so any excess heat from lighting will build up in the tank.

Nick,

I'm a reef convert to dart frogs, but I still have one small reef that's been stable for about 8 years.

The frogs are like fish, they don't really have specific lighting requirements. The nice thing about frog vivariums (vivs) is that loosing power isn't a crisis.

That's a big tank and you could do a lot of cool stuff with it. This site and others have a lot of "how to" info related to building out various types of vivs. You should look into substrate (soil) recipes that include clay components. It's the latest theory on keeping a healthy population of microfauna in the leaf litter to supplement the frog's diet. It's kind of like the shift from bare bottom reefs to aerobic/anaerobic coral sand bottom reefs. In fact, the parallels are striking.
