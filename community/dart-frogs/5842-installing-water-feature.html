Most people will create a false bottom, aswell as a false background. This means that you will have a section on the back that allows you to stick you hand all the way into the bottom to reach the pump. Basically make a 4x4" verticle box (or whatever size you preffer) down one of the rear corners of the tank. Then all you have to do is cover this square up using the foam. The top is left open and the vivarium lid will act as the top to this hole so nothing can get inside. You could also add something over the hole if the top doesnt quite seel it good enough, using mesh or something.

1st of all these are quite loaded questions and they are a ton of answers out there. Best place for you to start would be to look through all the "stickie" threads on this site, aswell as go onto Dendroboad.com. It has an insane amount of information, vivarium ideas, equipment, care sheets for each species, ect ect ect...

Now that I have said what everyone else is thinking I'll try my best to answer some of the questions.

Mixing species is a big no-no, especially for a beginner. A colony is fine as long as a food source is always in constant supply, but dont mix species. There is a thread on here that was recently started on this issue. Search for "mixing species" in the search bar and it should come up. And for a 40 galon I dont think anymore then 3-4 should be housed. If you start with a thumbnail species, maybe 5-6.

There are tons of different background methods and I cannot list them all. Keep on checking through the build threads and you will figure out what you think is within your skills range, and what is visually appealing to you. Also, try and decide which frogs you would like to start out with before starting to build. Some species require height and climbing areas, whereas others need plenty of ground area.

Whichever substrate you plan on going with, make sure to add springtails, and other buggers into it before introducing any darts. This will act as an extra "in tank" food supply in addition to the fruit flies being fed.

I have no expirence with darts at the moment, but so far though my own research I have picked up some valuable information. Like I said before, Dendroboards has a rediculous amount of information pertaining to dart frog housing and care.

The Binder?titebond method or Silicone
Quote:
Originally Posted by froggsong
First, I don't think you guys are understanding me on how the concrete binder or titebond is used. You don't use it like silicone, you mix it together with whatever you want to use for the BG (sand, peat, coco fiber, etc) and then spread it on similar to concrete, except you do it in a single layer.



I was taking about using it for the outside of the topper, no holes would be necessary, it would only form the rigid base for the foam.



I was referring to the concrete binder method, not concrete. The purpose of letting it dry and then wetting it when you use the binder method is to leach out the excess binder. This makes the structure stronger.

And again:


No, I haven't tried the titebond. I have, however, tried the binder. It works the same way, it's just that the titebond is waterproof, allowing you to use it on water features.

If you want to put broms in the foam, you'll have to use staples or glue, or put them in before you cover the BG in the mixture.

It looks MUCH more realistic. It doesn't look like you glued stuff onto a piece of foam. It looks much more realistic. With the silicone method, so many things can go wrong, and even when nothing does, it still erodes, especially if exposed to running water or constant misting (or even occasional high pressure misting). The coco fiber/moss/whatever is going to wash off, and then you'll just see the icky silicone underneath. It's time consuming, you have to work fast, and it takes a LONG time to cure. The binder/titebond method can even replace the concrete method, since you can use it with sand. Using sand will make it turn out just like concrete, without having to leach it or keep it moist so it will properly cure. With the binder/titebond method, if it cracks while drying, you can just patch it, I'm not sure how well that works with concrete. I'll see if I can get a picture of the small piece I am using with the binder mix on it. It just looks sooooooooo much more natural than silicone.

Now, just to clarify (since I think you guys got it wrong to begin with), with the bonder/titebond method, you MIX the binder/titebond with peat/coco fiber/sand/soil/etc and if too thick, a little water, then you spread it on your foam (it needs to have the skin removed first if you're using GS) and shape it like you would with concrete. It sticks in one layer because the binder and titebond are both essentially glues (and the titebond IS a glue, lol). It's much fast than the other methods; you can dry it overnight with a fan, then wet it throughly to leach the excess binder (shouldn't be necessary with the titebond, another reason I like it), then dry it again. After that, it's ready for plants and inhabitants. Two days max, from start to finish (not counting foam curing times, of course).

You can also mix in ground moss/moss spores and they should eventually grow as long as you keep the humidity high and mist the BG from time to time. If you're using a fogger, that's even better.

Please let me know if I need to clarify that better. I really think you guys had it all wrong to begin with. You don't put the glue on the foam and then press anything into it, you mix it in a bucket.

Google flevopol to see examples and to get a recipe (I do have one somewhere, I'll just have to locate it). Beck, the guy in your favorite link used this method... I didn't think you'd misunderstand what I was talking about...
