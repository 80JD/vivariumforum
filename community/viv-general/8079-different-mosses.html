The worst thing that could happen is you bring in parisites or the moss has been exposed to some sort of chemical.

Now that we have that out of the way, my advice before you put moss that you found out side in to a tank with animals grow it in a grow out tank. Set up a small 5 or 10 galon, with lights and water, then keep the humidity up, wait a few.months and you should notice some growth.

I work out side alot and some.of my favorit house to work on have ceader shake roofs, in alberta and BC. with the right conditions, east faceing roof, some trees over hanging the roof and some organic matter. You should get some nice moss growth. Now I usualy grab some.of the.moss that is growing on these roofs and place them ceader and moss in to my viv. Usualy takes about a month to notice any real growth.

Now my second tip is do a little research on moss, its not actualy a plant, in the sense it.does not have.roots, it likes to have a moist enviroment, so keep the wood that its atached to or rock moist.
One more thing this is where most people go wrong, moss is a picky lighting type of creature. It likes a redder spectrum light. I have found that halogens are the.best to grow moss under. I know there are heat and power issues with halogens, but they seem to work for me.

To answer your question about mixing mosses, there isn't really any downside. You are definitely right about moss shooting spores to adapt to a new environment. They usually do this when going to submerged to emmersed or vise versa. Mixing moss is a great way to show contrast in your vivarium. So it does serve a form but no function.

I would definitely quarantine the moss first before introducing it in your tank. Just to remove hitchhikers.

Now my second tip is do a little research on moss, its not actualy a plant, in the sense it.
Moss is what is known as a non-vascular plant. They do have a root structure but no vascular system like most terrestrial plants. The reason they needs so much moisture has more to do with osmotic pressure keeping the plant hydrated and firm. Not having a vascular system is also what makes them so short. Think of it as the first plant that crawled onto land from algae ancestors (who are microbes not plants).

I always stay away from outdoor plants for the same pest issues. I buy my moss in a hibernation state so comes with no soil or bugs. Mist them with water and they spring back to life in about 5 minutes.

Mixing species really won't do anything. They can't cross pollinate and one will not over run the other. Moss behave less like terrestrial plants and more like algae in that sense. Most ground covers will choke out terrestrial more than each other. Kind of like how you use wood chips to discourage weed growth (grass is a weed too). But most mosses do not like to neighbor right up against a plant. Too much competition for water, nutrients, and photons (light needed for cleaving water into molecules to make ATP and starch).
