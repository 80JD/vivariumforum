Even worse, "common" names are not common worldwide. Hence, the use of "pothos"--as Epipremnum aureum is known in the U.S.--may be meaningless and make the plant impossible to find in, for example, Canada (I ran into this exact problem on another forum recently). The use of "devil's ivy," another "common" name for E. aureum, makes some people believe it is an ivy, while others believe pothos is a philodendron.

Using a botanical name helps prevent such confusion, and it helps people ensure that they are purchasing/using a plant that is not harmful to any herps in an enclosure.

Staying with the pothos example, if it is thought to be an ivy or a philodendron, one would not want to include it in vivarium with herps. However, knowing that it is an Epipremnum, one can learn that it is not harmful to herps.

Finally, if a botanical name is posted, one can easily copy, paste, and google that name and learn all of the common names as well as detailed care & environmental info, whereas the use of a "common" name (that isn't common after all) may simply drive someone crazy as they try to figure out what the other person is talking about.

In sum, I vote for continued use of botanical names, with inclusion of known common names when possible for nonbotanists (either by training or in an amateur sense).

Finally, those of you who rely on common names need to consider the country of the person posting when seeking a plant in your local area by a common name, so go armed with the botanical name as a backup plan.
