Substrate layers for a dart frog vivarium, from drainage to leaf litter

Written November 2026 by Lone Star Leaf Litter, the family business that keeps this archive online.

We sell Texas live oak leaf litter, which is the top layer described below. Weigh what we say about that layer with this in mind. We do not sell anything used in the other layers.

Why the floor is built in layers

A dart frog vivarium is misted often and has nowhere for the water to go. If the floor were one deep bed of soil, the bottom would fill with water, lose its air and turn sour. Plant roots rot in that, and it is not a healthy surface for frogs to live on.

Layers fix this by giving the water a place to collect that is separate from the soil. From the bottom up, the usual build is:

  1. a drainage layer
  2. a barrier
  3. the substrate
  4. an optional layer of sphagnum moss
  5. leaf litter

The drainage layer

The drainage layer is a bed of coarse, inert material with large gaps between the pieces. Water runs through the soil above and collects here.

Most keepers use LECA, the lightweight fired-clay balls sold for hydroponics. Gravel does the same job and is named as an alternative in published build guides, but it is far heavier, which matters in a large glass tank on a stand. Rinse whichever you use before it goes in, because the dust clouds the water.

Two to three inches is the depth most guides give, and at least one supplier asks for three as a minimum. A deeper layer buys you more time between drainings.

Some keepers skip the loose fill and build a raised platform out of plastic light diffuser panel, often called egg crate. Several of the forum threads linked at the end compare the two approaches.

The barrier

A sheet of mesh sits on top of the drainage layer and keeps the soil from washing down into it. Fiberglass window screen is the usual material. Purpose-made substrate barrier is also sold.

Cut it a little larger than the floor of the tank and press the extra up against the glass. A barrier cut to the exact size leaves gaps at the edges, and soil finds them.

Water in the drainage layer

One rule is agreed on by everyone: the water in the drainage layer must never reach the barrier or the soil above it. Once it does, the soil wicks it up and stays waterlogged.

Keepers disagree on how much water should sit down there. Some keep a shallow pool in the bottom on purpose, to help hold humidity. Others drain it whenever it builds up. Either works as long as the level stays well below the barrier.

Plan how you will get the water out before you add anything else. The usual method is to leave one front corner free of soil, held back with stones or cork, so you can see the level and reach it with a siphon hose.

Look at the level through the front glass whenever you mist. If it is getting near the barrier, drain it that day.

The substrate

The substrate is where the plants root and where most of the springtails and isopods live. It has to hold moisture, drain freely and keep its structure for years in constant humidity.

The best-known recipe is ABG mix, named for the Atlanta Botanical Garden. The classic version, by volume:

  • 2 parts tree fern fiber
  • 2 parts fir or orchid bark
  • 1 part peat moss
  • 1 part charcoal
  • 1 part sphagnum moss

It is sold ready mixed, and many suppliers sell their own blends built on the same idea.

Keepers do not agree on every ingredient. Some avoid peat, either because it is acidic and breaks down sooner than the rest or because it is not a renewable material, and use coconut fiber in its place. One published recipe leaves ABG behind altogether: two parts coconut husk fiber, one part coconut husk chunks and one part milled sphagnum, with bark or tree fern fiber as optional extras. What the working recipes have in common is a lot of coarse, chunky material that keeps air in the mix.

What to avoid is more settled. Garden topsoil and ordinary potting soil are fine-textured, pack down and break down quickly in a wet tank, and bagged potting soil may contain fertilizer or other additives that do not belong near amphibian skin.

Two to three inches of substrate is a common starting point. Dampen it before it goes in. It should hold together when you squeeze a handful and should not drip. Dry peat-based mixes shed water at first, so this step saves trouble later.

A well-drained substrate usually lasts for years. If one area does go sour, you can dig out and replace that section and leave the rest of the tank alone.

The optional sphagnum layer

Some keepers lay half an inch to an inch of soaked long-fiber sphagnum moss over the substrate before the leaves go on. It gives a soft, damp surface between the frogs and the coarse mix and holds moisture near the top. Other keepers put the leaves straight onto the substrate. Both are in regular use.

Leaf litter on top

The last layer is dried leaves, and for dart frogs it is the layer that matters most day to day. The frogs hide and hunt in it. Springtails and isopods breed under it and feed on the leaves as they soften. It also means the frogs sit on leaves and not on wet soil.

Live oak, magnolia and sea grape are the leaves most often used. All three are thick and leathery, so they hold their shape in humidity for a long time. Live oak leaves are small, which suits small frogs and small tanks. Magnolia and sea grape are large and make roomy hides.

Use leaves that are dry and brown and that come from ground you know has not been sprayed. If you would sooner buy them, this is the part we sell: hand-collected Texas live oak leaves, sorted and screened. Screening is not sterilization, so look through any bag before you use it, ours included.

Lay the leaves loosely and deep enough to hide most of the soil. Our page on how to use leaf litter in an enclosure covers how much to add. The layer gets eaten and breaks down, so top it up when bare patches show.

Before the frogs go in

Add springtails and isopods once the layers are down and the tank is planted, and give them a few weeks with nothing hunting them. That time also lets you watch how fast the drainage layer fills with your misting routine, and adjust it before any animals depend on it.
